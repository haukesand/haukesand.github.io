Nobody reads privacy policies, and the people who do tend to read into them what they expect to find. For the graduate course Privacy in the Digital Age at Cornell Tech, taught by Helen Nissenbaum and Vitaly Shmatikov, I turned a decade of in-class policy analysis into a structured canvas that pairs of students can complete in one class period.

The canvas has three panels. Basic information about the service and the policy (length, last update, who is covered, which information types), with a trust rating taken before the analysis. An information flow panel where students pick five flows from the policy and annotate them with the contextual integrity parameters (sender, recipient, subject, information type, transmission principle), marking what is missing and highlighting vague language. And a detailed analysis panel with nine practice dimensions: collection, third parties, sharing, retention, scope, consent, transparency, use, and a box for odd practices, followed by a second trust rating. The template lives in FigJam and was translated into Chinese, Dutch, German, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, and Spanish.

In Fall 2024, 59 students analyzed 31 policies with it. Everyone finished within class time, and the odd-practices box did most of the teaching: pixel tracking in emails, voice emotion detection, retention of deleted content. Trust in established tech companies fell after the analysis while trust in emerging services rose slightly. I presented the canvas as a poster at NYC Privacy Day 2025 {% cite Sandhaus2025-canvas %}. The write-up was submitted as a work-in-progress paper and not accepted; the PDF is linked above as is.
