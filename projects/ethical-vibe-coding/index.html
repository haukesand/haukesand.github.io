Ethical Vibe Coding

A three-week course for high school students who build real apps with AI coding tools while learning to spot bias, dark patterns, and their own responsibility.

Statusfinished; course ran July 2026
CourseTECHIE 1121, Cornell Tech Summer Innovation Intensives
WithJonathan Segal, Wendy Ju
Course site Summer Innovation Intensives

Good code, good vibes. TECHIE 1121 was a three-week course in Cornell Tech’s Summer Innovation Intensives for high school students with no programming background. Jonathan Segal and I designed and taught it, with Wendy Ju. Students built hosted web applications with AI coding assistants from day one, and every day ended with a written reflection.

Week one, Vibe Coding: Gaining Control, covered the tools: a personal portfolio page, prompting strategies that steer the output, and a session on bias in vibe coding where students probe which names, stock images, and defaults the tools produce when nobody specifies them. Week two, Ethics: Doing the Right Thing, turned the tools on themselves: values in vibe coding with the Values at Play framework, a red-teaming day where students build dark patterns on purpose in order to recognize them, and AI Against AI, using AI to defend against AI harms. Week three was a multi-day group project.

Each in-class activity has a live page, instructions, and a reflection rubric, so the course site doubles as a reusable curriculum. The course grew out of my research on how students use generative AI in HCI prototyping classes (Sandhaus et al., 2025) (Parreira et al., 2026).

  1. CHIWORK Workshop
    Visible Adoption, Untracked Contribution: GitHub Evidence of the Accountability Gap Across Three Cohorts of an HCI Prototyping Course
    Maria Teresa Parreira, Pranav Prabhat Sinha, Hauke Sandhaus, and 1 more author
    In CHIWORK ’26: Adjunct Proceedings of the 5th Annual Symposium on Human-Computer Interaction for Work, Linz, Austria, Jun 2026
    Workshop paper at CHIWORK ’26 (workshop I co-organized)
    Abs arXiv Bib

    This paper presents a longitudinal, observational case study of how student GenAI adoption shifted across three cohorts (Fall 2022, 2023, and 2025) of the same graduate-level HCI prototyping course, using computational analysis of 203 GitHub repositories with student activity and 23,065 student commits. Building on a prior qualitative study of the 2023 cohort, we distinguish two levels of AI accountability trace: disclosure (naming that an AI tool was used) and attribution (crediting a specific artifact or task to an AI tool). We find that tool disclosure grew from 0% to 66% of repositories across the three cohorts, while explicit contribution attribution remains a minority practice, and the gap between the two reveals where accountability is missing even among students who disclose. By 2025, AI is infrastructure embedded in course templates and student-built devices: students increasingly name the tools they used, but rarely specify what those tools contributed. We argue that disclosure-based frameworks are insufficient for the vibe-coding era. The failure is not that students conceal AI use; it is that a norm built for episodic, identifiable acts cannot capture continuous, ambient co-creation. We offer this case study as grounding for the workshop’s conversation about what genuine co-thinking accountability looks like.

    TLDR: Analysis of 203 GitHub repos across three HCI-course cohorts shows disclosure of AI-tool use grew to 66%, but explicit contribution attribution stayed rare — revealing an accountability gap in the vibe-coding era.

  1. DIS
    Co-Designing with Transformers: Unpacking the Complex Role of GenAI in Interactive System Design Education
    Hauke Sandhaus, Qiuquan Gu, Maria Teresa Parreira, and 1 more author
    In Proceedings of the 2025 ACM Designing Interactive Systems Conference, Funchal, Portugal, Jul 2025
    Abs arXiv Bib HTML PDF Slides

    Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is transforming Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) education and technology design, yet its impact remains poorly understood. This study explores how graduate students in an applied HCI course used GenAI tools during interactive device design. Despite no encouragement, all groups integrated GenAI into their workflows. Through 12 post-class group interviews, we identified how GenAI co-design behaviors present both benefits, such as enhanced creativity and faster design iterations, and risks, including shallow learning and reflection. Benefits were most evident during the execution phases, while the discovery and reflection phases showed limited gains. A taxonomy of usage patterns revealed that students’ outcomes depended more on how they used GenAI than the specific tasks performed. These findings highlight the need for HCI education to adapt to GenAI’s role and offer recommendations for curricula to better prepare future designers for effective creative co-design.

    TLDR: GenAI in Human-Computer Interaction education and design brings both benefits, such as enhanced creativity and faster iterations, and risks, including shallow learning and reflection. Students’ approach to GenAI, rather than the specific tasks performed, influences the success of GenAI co-design.
