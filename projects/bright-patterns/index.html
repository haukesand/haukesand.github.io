Bright Patterns

Defines the term and collects benevolent design solutions that put user goals and well-being first.

Statusmaintaining since 2023
Rolecreator and maintainer
Sitebrightpatterns.org
Website Submit a pattern Workshop paper

Dark patterns have a name, a taxonomy, and a regulatory audience. Their opposite did not. In a CHI ‘23 workshop paper (Sandhaus, 2023) I proposed bright patterns as benevolent design solutions that prioritize user goals and well-being, even where that works against a company’s short-term interest. Brightpatterns.org is the public front page for that term.

The site has three parts. A Hall of Fame of fourteen named patterns, such as Nutrition Label, Escape Hatch, Slow Down, and Usage Limits, each with a description and real examples. A Discoveries in the Wild section for patterns people spot in products. And a submission form, so the collection grows through nominations rather than only through my own reading.

The site also carries a citation block for people who use the term in papers or policy work. I update it when new submissions arrive and when my own work on UX evaluation metrics (Sandhaus et al., 2026) shifts how I think about the taxonomy.

    Making Indecent Persuasion Visible: How Evaluation Metrics Shape UX Designers’ Ethical Reasoning
    Hauke Sandhaus, Doris Maria Rhomberg, and Helen Nissenbaum
    In CHIWORK ’26: Proceedings of the 5th Annual Symposium on Human-Computer Interaction for Work (Late-Breaking Work), Linz, Austria, Jun 2026
    Best LBW Award Abs Bib HTML PDF Poster

    Best Late-Breaking Work Award at CHIWORK ’26 (1 of 25)

    UX evaluation tools are widely treated as neutral measurement instruments, yet the metrics designers see during evaluation may determine what they can recognise as ethically problematic. In a three-condition experiment, 141 UX professionals evaluated social media interfaces containing dark patterns. When dashboards included persuasion-focused items alongside standard User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) data, designers rejected manipulative interfaces at nearly double the rate of a no-data baseline (56.2% vs. 30.0%). Standard UEQ data alone already produced a significant intermediate shift (43.9% rejection), so any user-testing signal recalibrates judgement. Decision confidence stayed uniformly high across conditions: designers without evaluation data were not cautiously uncertain but confidently uncalibrated, reaching different conclusions than they would have with user data while feeling equally sure. Analysis of 1,313 written justifications showed that persuasion-focused data shifted reasoning from business and aesthetic framings toward explicit engagement with manipulation, user trust, and autonomy. We introduce indecent persuasion, behavioural influence that users experience as pressuring, addictive, covert, or deceptive, as a dimension of user experience that standard instruments fail to surface. The findings raise a question about evaluation infrastructure: who decides which dimensions of user experience count?

    TLDR: In a study of 141 UX professionals, persuasion-focused evaluation metrics nearly doubled rejection of manipulative interfaces, surfacing "indecent persuasion" as a dimension standard UX instruments fail to capture.

  1. CHI Workshop
    Promoting Bright Patterns
    Hauke Sandhaus
    In CHI ’23 Workshop: Designing Technology and Policy Simultaneously, Hamburg, Germany, Apr 2023
    This paper started the brightpatterns.org website
    Abs arXiv Bib HTML Website

    User experience designers are facing increasing scrutiny and criticism for creating harmful technologies, leading to a pushback against unethical design practices. While clear-cut harmful practices such as dark patterns have received attention, trends towards automation, personalization, and recommendation present more ambiguous ethical challenges. To address potential harm in these "gray" instances, we propose the concept of "bright patterns" - persuasive design solutions that prioritize user goals and well-being over their desires and business objectives. The ambition of this paper is threefold: to define the term "bright patterns", to provide examples of such patterns, and to advocate for the adoption of bright patterns through policymaking.

    TLDR: The first definition of "bright patterns" is accompanied by examples that illustrate corporate use of persuasive design in support of user goals over their desires and business objectives.
